When a Fix Works, But the Pattern of Needing It Doesn't

The task: detect a playing card in a live phone camera feed in real time, then overlay different graphics onto it, tracking its position as it moved. The approach was classical computer vision — no machine learning, just geometric and color-based rules applied frame by frame: find bright or edge-defined regions shaped like a rectangle, exclude skin-toned regions so a hand holding the card wouldn't get mistaken for part of it, then warp the overlay onto whatever shape was found.

This worked. Each individual problem that came up got solved, and solved correctly, with evidence to back it:

- The detected region was consistently bigger than the real card -> traced to camera overexposure bleeding past the card's true edge -> fixed with image processing to pull the boundary back in.
- A hand gripping the card got absorbed into the detected shape, growing the overlay to include part of the hand -> fixed by identifying and removing skin-colored pixels before fitting any shape at all.
- The same settings that worked during the day failed at night -> traced (with real sensor-data logging, not guesswork) to the camera's auto white-balance shifting color readings when the bright card entered frame -> fixed by locking the camera's exposure and white balance once the scene settled.
- Even with that fix, a new problem appeared: when only a small sliver of the card was visible (just entering frame), its color reading became unreliable — simply because a tiny, dim patch of pixels in low light doesn't carry much reliable color information. No threshold adjustment fixes that; it's a property of how small, dim regions behave, not a number to tune.

That last one is the tell. By that point, four rounds of real, data-backed fixes had each worked — but each one only covered the specific condition it was tested under. Every round surfaced a new edge case rather than running out of them. That's a different situation than "we haven't found the right setting yet." It's a signal that the chosen approach — fixed numeric rules, hand-picked from whatever conditions you tested in — doesn't generalize to a problem with this much real-world variability (lighting, distance, motion, sensor noise), no matter how carefully each rule is tuned.

The alternative worth naming: a trained model, shown many real examples instead of hand-picked thresholds, learns to recognize the shape of the problem rather than memorizing the exact brightness and color values of one test session. That's a bigger undertaking — collecting real examples, training, and re-integrating — not a quick swap. But it's the right kind of bigger: solving the actual problem instead of chasing its latest symptom.

The broader lesson: if your fourth fix in a row is still "found a new condition the rule doesn't cover," that's not a tuning problem anymore. The rule-based system is telling you it was never going to generalize — and no fifth fix changes that.
